AI Training in Nepal: A Practical Guide for Teams

AI training works in Nepal when it starts with a real piece of work, not a list of tools. This guide covers the formats, a useful curriculum, the questions to ask, and how to tell whether the course changed anything.

NATDS Field Notes · Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Searches for AI courses in Nepal usually return a long list of tool demos. That is a reasonable starting point, but a tool demo is not training. Training changes behaviour: a manager starts checking evidence in a decision brief, a researcher keeps a record of AI use, and an operations team stops retyping the same report every week.

Start with the decision, not the tool

Before choosing a course, write down one recurring task that costs your team time. It might be a monthly report, a set of meeting notes, a stack of policy documents people keep asking about, or a batch of applicant questions. A good course will use that task as its example. If a provider cannot explain how the day connects to your work, keep looking.

We use the same sequence with every NATDS group: map the week, pick one workflow, build it with review, then hand it back to the team with a short implementation plan.

The three formats you will find in Nepal

  • Classroom training in Kathmandu — useful for teams that can travel and want to work on shared examples in the room.
  • Live online training — the same sessions delivered live, with breaks and exercises, for teams across Nepal or across branches.
  • In-house or on-site workshops — delivered at your office, using non-confidential examples from your own work. This is often the best option for a whole department.

The format matters less than the exercise. Ask what participants will actually build, and what they will take back to their desk on Monday.

What a practical curriculum should cover

A useful AI course in Nepal does not need to be technical. It does need to be specific. Look for these five elements:

  • Document question-and-answer over files your team already uses.
  • Meeting notes turned into action lists with owners and deadlines.
  • One-page decision briefs with evidence that can be checked.
  • A simple rule for what data may be shared with a tool and who reviews the output.
  • A 30-day plan for one workflow, owned by the people who do the work.

Responsible use is not a separate lecture at the end. It belongs inside each exercise: what was uploaded, what was checked, and who remains accountable.

Five questions to ask before you enrol

  • Will we work on our own non-confidential examples, or only generic demos?
  • How many sessions are there, and how much time is between them?
  • What will each participant be able to do after the final session?
  • How do you handle data, review and disclosure in a Nepali organisation?
  • What happens after training if we get stuck?

If the answers stay at the level of "we cover ChatGPT", the course is probably an introduction to tools rather than training for work.

How to measure the result

Pick one measure before the course starts. It can be hours spent on a weekly report, the time from meeting to action list, or the number of people who can run a document workflow without help. Measure it again two weeks after training. A course that cannot point to a changed workflow has not finished its job.

Frequently asked questions

How long should practical AI training take? For a team, eight two-hour sessions over several weeks is a workable start. It gives people time to try a workflow at work and return with real questions. A single day can introduce tools, but it rarely changes how a team operates.

Do we need technical staff? No. Managers, operations staff, researchers and teachers can all use AI safely once they know what may be shared, how to check an output, and which workflow to improve first.

Should training be at our office or online? Both work. In-house sessions help a team work on its own documents together. Live online training suits teams in different cities and can follow the same curriculum.
